Dev reference — pitch diagram component

Not part of the app UI. This documents the two scales, the four pattern hues, and the fill convention used everywhere pitch appears (review card, Edit word sheet).

Compact scale — inline in a review-card row
飲む — atamadaka. Mora 1 high, mora 2 low — height alone carries the drop, read together with the connecting line. No kana underneath — the vocabulary's own ruby already shows its lemma reading. Reused verbatim on the review card's vocab row.
Fill: normal mora vs. nasal mora
Filled dot — normal mora. The default for every mora. Fill no longer encodes high/low — that's the dot's vertical position and the contour line, above.
Empty dot — nasal mora (鼻濁音, the prenasalized が-row heard mid-word in careful/NHK-style Tokyo speech). Same pattern hue, hollow instead of filled, at whatever height its pitch already sits at.
音楽 — heiban, mora 3 (が) nasal. Illustrative only — the pinned Kanjium accents.txt carries pitch positions but no nasal/devoicing markers, so today this diagram renders nasal when the source supplies it and stays all-filled otherwise. Gated in SPEC.md §15.
Full scale — Edit word sheet
飲む — atamadaka. Same word, full scale: bordered block per mora, kana printed under its own dot. No pattern name, no accent number — the reading is the only label.
上手 — atamadaka (one of two listed patterns for this spelling).
上手 — heiban, the other listed pattern. Both appear together in the Edit word sheet's pick-list (screen 23); which is active uses a neutral outline and check, not pitch color alone.
Pattern-color reference only — not a looked-up word
Nakadaka hue (drop somewhere in the middle) — preserve every mora; a long diagram scrolls horizontally instead of shrinking kana or truncating the word.
Odaka hue — a short trailing drop marks the word boundary, unlike heiban's continuing high contour. The tail is not another mora or a prediction of sentence intonation.
Missing pitch — no Kanjium match

No diagram at all — just the small neutral mark, in place of where the diagram would sit. Same mark used for an ambiguous (more-than-one-pattern) auto-pick and for a locally-translated sentence; context tells you which.
